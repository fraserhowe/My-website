Key Points
  • A covered double-digit income. UKW pays a CPI-linked dividend yielding about 10%, covered 1.3 times by cash in 2025 and guided to 1.7 times in 2026. This dividend has risen for twelve consecutive years.
  • Assets are priced above where the market values the shares. The shares trade 22% below the 134.2p net asset value. In 2025 the company sold £181m of wind farms at their carrying value, and a rival fund, Bluefield Solar, has agreed to be taken over near its own NAV — both data points suggesting the underlying asset marks are being validated by real transactions even as the share price sits well below them.
  • The discount is sector-wide, not company-specific. Higher interest rates re-rated every renewable trust from premiums to wide discounts. Within its wind peer group UKW trades at the narrowest discount and carries the strongest cover.
  • The catalysts that would close the gap haven't yet appeared. A recovery in the share price would need lower rates or firmer power prices. The Bank of England held rates in June with two members voting to raise them, and the long-term power price outlook is soft. NAV fell in 2024 and 2025 before stabilising in Q1 2026.
  • Scenario modelling. A base case holding NAV broadly flat and the discount near current levels implies a share value close to where it sits today. A scenario where power prices and sentiment recover points to a higher implied share value; further power-price downgrades and a wider discount, to a lower one.

Business Overview

UKW listed in 2013 as the first renewable infrastructure trust in London and is now a FTSE 250 member with a market value of about £2.1bn. It owns interests in 49 operating UK wind farms totalling 1.9 GW, split by value across England (47%), Scotland (43%), Northern Ireland (8%) and Wales (2%). The company purchases completed, revenue-generating wind farms rather than building them, collects the cash they produce, and distributes most of it as a quarterly dividend that targets growth in line with CPI inflation.

It is externally managed by Schroders Greencoat, Europe's largest specialist renewable manager, for an ongoing charge of 0.83%. The board is independent, chaired by Lucinda Riches. There is no controlling shareholder: like most UK investment trusts, UKW is widely held by retail investors and wealth managers.

Because it is closed-ended, the company has a fixed number of shares that trade on the exchange at whatever price the market sets, which can sit well above or below the value of the underlying assets, rather than issuing or redeeming shares at asset value like an open-ended fund.

Revenue comes from three sources: electricity sold at market prices, government subsidies attached to most of the portfolio, and fixed-price power contracts. About 68% of cash flow is fixed over the next twelve months, giving near-term visibility, though this proportion falls over time as older subsidies expire and the portfolio becomes more exposed to the open-market power price. One structural change is worth noting: from April 2026 the Renewables Obligation, the portfolio's main inflation-linked subsidy, switched from RPI to CPI indexation, and the dividend target moved with it. UKW now assumes CPI of about 2.4–2.5% against RPI of 3.1–3.5%, so the annual uplift on both the dividend and the index-linked revenue is roughly a point lower than before. This trims future growth in the dividend and the NAV, though the inflation linkage itself remains intact.

NAV mechanics

The manager values each wind farm by discounting its projected lifetime cash flows — an asset-by-asset DCF. These sum to the gross portfolio value; debt and costs are deducted to reach the net asset value of 134.2p per share. Two inputs dominate: the discount rate, currently 9% unlevered and 11% levered, and the long-term power-price curve from an external consultant. The NAV is therefore an estimate, and sensitive — a 0.5 point rise in the discount rate cuts NAV by about 6%. UKW's 9% unlevered rate is higher, and so more conservative, than several peers use to value their assets.

The quarterly NAV walk shows the moving parts clearly. In Q1 2026, inflation and power prices added value while a higher discount rate and a subsidy change subtracted it — leaving NAV marginally higher.

Industry Overview

UKW sits among listed funds that own renewable assets and pay high incomes. The group was popular when rates were near zero and often traded above NAV; once a safe government bond began yielding around 4.5%, the sector re-rated to wide discounts. That repricing — driven by rates and sentiment rather than the wind farms — is the main reason the discount exists.

The longer-term swing factor is the power price. As clean power expands, the share of hours in which gas sets the wholesale price has fallen from over 90% in 2021 to around 60% today, and is expected near 15% by 2030. This lowers exposure to gas shocks but presses down the long-term prices wind farms achieve — the principal cause of the NAV falls in 2024 and 2025.

What Would Move the Picture

Catalysts

  • Sector takeovers near NAV. Bluefield Solar's agreed Drax takeover and BBGI's take-private both closed near asset value, and each such deal further validates UKW's own marks.
  • Supportive government policy. Clean Power 2030 positions wind as the backbone of the electricity system, and the government has ruled out zonal pricing, which would have disadvantaged Scottish wind.
  • Grid build-out. Up to £90bn of planned UK transmission investment and connection-queue reform would, if delivered, ease the Scotland–England bottleneck that constrains 43% of UKW's portfolio by value.
  • Voluntary CfDs. An April 2026 proposal to offer long-term fixed-price contracts to existing ROC-subsidised generators, with allocation planned for 2027, would directly reduce the power-price uncertainty weighing on NAV if UKW's assets are admitted.
  • Continued buybacks below NAV. £199m completed to date, adding 2.1p to NAV; repurchases below NAV mechanically narrow the discount as they continue.

Risks & Mitigants

  • A further fall in power prices. The cause of the last two NAV declines.
  • Capture-price erosion. As more wind capacity is built, output increasingly sells into the same windy hours.
  • A renewed energy-price shock. One that pushes the Bank of England toward rate rises rather than cuts; NAV is modelled to fall ~6% per 0.5pp rise in the discount rate.
  • The discount persisting. Renewable-trust discounts have stayed wide for several years.
  • The Electricity Generator Levy rising 45% to 55% from July 2026. UKW's 2025 captured price of £70/MWh sits below the £82.61/MWh threshold, so the company has stated this is not material at current levels.
  • A shrinking asset base. If buybacks stay funded by disposals, though the board has indicated a measured return to acquisitions from 2026.

Conclusion

Greencoat UK Wind offers a well-covered income of approximately 10%, and a discount to NAV that recent third-party transactions suggest is wider than the underlying assets alone would justify.

A narrowing of that discount depends largely on factors outside the company's control — interest-rate direction and power-price sentiment — neither of which currently points toward a near-term re-rating, with rates held and the power-price outlook soft. The income component of returns appears the more dependable element on current data; any capital recovery is more contingent. Things that would shift this picture include a turn in power-price forecasts, allocation of voluntary CfDs to UKW's ROC assets in 2027, a takeover approach for the company itself, or — on the downside — a further NAV downgrade, cover falling below ~1.2x, or a halt to the disposals currently validating the asset marks.

On a longer view, wind and solar economics look structurally favourable: the technology is mature, costs are established, and the energy-security case has been reinforced twice in three years by fossil-fuel shocks. The near-term picture is more volatile, with policy, power prices, and the broader emissions-reduction landscape all still moving. The UK and EU combined account for roughly 8% of global emissions, yet bear a large share of the regulatory and investment burden of the transition — a mismatch that keeps political risk elevated relative to the underlying asset quality.
